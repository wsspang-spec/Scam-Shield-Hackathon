# Taxonomy of US Phone Scams and Their Scripts (2024-2026)

Scope note: US-focused. Data drawn primarily from FTC Consumer Sentinel releases (2024 data released March 2025; 2025 data released March and June 2026), the FBI IC3 2024 and 2025 Internet Crime Reports, FTC Data Spotlights, FBI PSAs, industry reports (Hiya, Pindrop), and academic scam-call corpora. Where a source is a secondary summary (law-firm blog, news article) rather than the primary release, it is noted.

## Key Question 1: Top phone scam categories by losses and report volume (FTC 2024/2025, FBI IC3 2024/2025)

### Takeaway
Reported US fraud losses hit records in 2025: $15.9B to the FTC (3M reports) and $20.9B to the FBI IC3 (1.0M complaints). Imposter scams are the most reported category (over 1M FTC reports, $3.5B) and the dominant phone-delivered category; investment/crypto scams carry the largest dollar losses ($7.9B FTC, $8.6B IC3) but mostly start on social media. For a call-focused detector, the highest-impact archetypes by dollars are: bank/business impersonation, government impersonation (SSA/FTC/law enforcement, including jury duty and the gold/cash-courier variant), tech support and its "Phantom Hacker" evolution, and family emergency/grandparent, with Medicare and Amazon/package calls dominating by volume.

### Cited Findings

**FTC Consumer Sentinel, 2025 data (released 2026)**
- 2025: consumers submitted 3 million fraud reports and reported $15.9 billion in losses, up from 2.6 million reports and over $12 billion in 2024; reported losses have "risen nearly 430% since 2020" — [FTC JEC testimony, March 2026 (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
- Imposter scams: over 1 million reports and over $3.5 billion in losses in 2025; investment scams: over $7.9 billion, about half of all 2025 reported losses, average individual loss over $10,000 — [FTC JEC testimony](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
- Imposter scam losses of $3.5B in 2025, up nearly threefold since 2020; nearly one in three fraud reports concerned imposter scams, more than any other category; business impersonation nearly $1 billion (up from $866M in 2024); government impersonation about $920 million (up from $789M in 2024); bank impersonators had the highest losses among business impersonators (no dollar figure given) — [FTC press release, June 2026](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025); corroborated by [KnowBe4 summary](https://blog.knowbe4.com/ftc-report-imposter-scams-record-losses)
- 2025 contact methods: text message was the top reported contact method; social media had the highest aggregate losses (over $2 billion in FY2025); bank payments had the highest aggregate losses, cryptocurrency second; credit card was the most frequently identified payment method — [FTC JEC testimony](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)
- 2025 share of loss reports by contact method (excluding reports without a contact method): website/app 31%, social media 28%, phone call 11%, email 10%, text 7%. For ages 80+, phone call was the top contact method and phone calls were the most costly contact method in aggregate for that age group — [FTC Data Spotlight, April 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2026/04/reported-losses-scams-social-media-eight-times-higher-2020)
- FTC noted "a sharp increase in the number of consumers reporting large losses of $100,000 or more" and estimated 2024 fraud costs could reach $195.9 billion accounting for underreporting — [MoneyTalksNews summary of FTC release](https://www.moneytalksnews.com/americans-lost-a-record-billion-to-scams-in-ftc-says/)

**FTC Consumer Sentinel, 2024 data (released March 2025)**
- 2024: more than $12.5 billion in reported losses (+25% YoY); 38% of fraud reporters lost money (up from 27% in 2023); investment scams $5.7B; imposter scams $2.95B; government imposter $789M (+$171M); phone calls were the second most common contact method after email; bank transfers and crypto losses exceeded all other payment methods combined — [FTC press release, March 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/03/new-ftc-data-show-big-jump-reported-losses-fraud-125-billion-2024)
- 2024 losses by contact method: social media ~$1.858B, websites/apps ~$976M, phone calls ~$948M, email ~$502M, text ~$470M, online ads/pop-ups ~$246M. By payment method: bank transfers/payments ~$2.089B, crypto ~$1.417B, payment apps ~$391M, cash ~$308M, wire ~$287M, credit cards ~$275M, gift/reload cards ~$212M. Imposter scam reports: 845,806 — [ABA Business Law Today (secondary, citing CSN data book)](https://businesslawtoday.org/2026/08/consumer-sentinel-data-as-a-business-law-tool-for-measuring-consumer-financial-harm/)
- 2024: people who lost money to phone scams lost around $1,500 each (reported as an average/typical figure; the article does not specify median) — [Bitdefender summary of FTC data](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/ftc-americans-lost-12-5-billion-to-scams-last-year)

**FBI IC3 2025 Internet Crime Report (released 2026)**
- 1,008,597 complaints (first year above 1M), $20.877 billion in losses (+26% YoY), average loss per complaint $20,699 — [SOCRadar summary of IC3 2025](https://socradar.io/blog/fbi-ic3-2025-internet-crime-report-10-takeaways/)
- Crime type table, 2025 vs 2024 (complaints / losses): Government Impersonation 32,424 / $797.9M vs 17,367 / $405.6M; Tech/Customer Support 47,794 / $2.135B vs 36,002 / $1.465B; Confidence/Romance 23,159 / $929.3M vs 17,910 / $672.0M; Extortion 89,129 / $122.5M vs 86,415 / $143.2M; Investment 72,984 / $8.649B vs 47,919 / $6.571B; Phishing/Spoofing 191,561 / $215.8M vs 193,407 / $70.0M; Lottery/Sweepstakes/Inheritance 5,623 / $194.1M vs 3,690 / $102.2M; Identity Theft 31,675 / $185.8M vs 21,403 / $174.4M — [FBI IC3 2025 Report (PDF)](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)
- "Call center fraud" (Tech/Customer Support plus Government Impersonation combined) totaled more than 80,000 complaints and losses exceeding $2.9 billion in 2025; FBI enabled ~175 arrests via 13 joint operations with India's CBI in 2025 — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)
- Crypto ATM/kiosk: 13,460 complaints, $389M in losses (+23% complaints, +58% losses YoY); age 60+ accounted for 6,188 complaints and $257.5M — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)
- Crypto-related investment schemes: $7.2B; BEC $3.046B; recovery scams $1.4B from 10,516 complaints; AI-referenced complaints: more than 22,000 with adjusted losses above $893M — [SOCRadar summary](https://socradar.io/blog/fbi-ic3-2025-internet-crime-report-10-takeaways/)
- Tech support losses trajectory: $924M (2023), $1.464B (2024), $2.134B (2025) — [SOCRadar summary](https://socradar.io/blog/fbi-ic3-2025-internet-crime-report-10-takeaways/)

**Industry volume data**
- Medicare scams were the most common US fraud call type in Q4 2024, peaking the week open enrollment ended; US average reported loss per fraud-call victim was $539 — [Hiya Q4 2024 press release](https://www.hiya.com/newsroom/press-releases/ai-deepfake-fraud-calls-dominate-q4-scams-costing-consumers-millions)
- Medicare scams "remained the #1 scam in the country" (US) in Q1 2025 — [Hiya Q1 2025 blog](https://blog.hiya.com/global-call-threat-report-q1-brings-a-new-assortment-of-phone-scams)
- Truecaller's US report (2021, dated but widely cited): ~59.5M Americans lost money to phone scams in the prior 12 months, ~$29.8B total, average loss $502, 60% of victims duped by robocalls — [TechSpot summary of Truecaller 2021](https://www.techspot.com/news/90702-phone-scammers-have-swindled-americans-out-nearly-30.html)

### Inferences
- For a call-focused detector, "imposter" is the umbrella category that matters most: it is the most-reported FTC category, and the two largest IC3 "call center fraud" lines (tech support, government impersonation) nearly doubled in losses between 2024 and 2025.
- Phone call's share of first-contact reports (11% in 2025) understates its dollar importance: phone-initiated fraud has the highest median loss of any channel for older adults ($2,210, see Q4) and is the top channel for 80+.
- Many scams reported under "social media" or "online ad/pop-up" are designed to migrate to a phone call (FTC explicitly says this), so a call detector will encounter investment and tech-support scams whose first contact was not a call.
- Investment/pig-butchering is the largest dollar category but is primarily text/social-first; the voice component usually comes later (WhatsApp calls), so it is lower priority for an inbound-call detector than impersonation.

### Gaps
- The FTC's 2025 Consumer Sentinel Data Book itself (with the full contact-method and payment-method tables, median losses by channel for 2025) was not directly retrieved; 2025 phone-call aggregate losses and median are therefore not in these notes. The 2024 equivalents are included.
- The FTC 2025 imposter press release does not separate tech support or family/friend impersonation; the IC3 figures fill that in for tech support only.
- No reliable 2024-2026 Truecaller US figure surfaced; only the 2021 report.

## Key Question 2: Script anatomy per major scam type (opening, claimed identity, crisis, demand, payment rail, duration, pushback handling)

### Takeaway
Across types, the script structure is remarkably stable: (1) authority-establishing greeting, often with a case/reference number; (2) a manufactured problem (unauthorized charge, SSN linked to crime, hacked computer, arrested relative, missed jury duty, unpaid bill); (3) identity "verification" that harvests PII; (4) a handoff to a "supervisor," "fraud department," or second agency; (5) isolation instructions ("do not tell anyone," "stay on the line"); (6) extraction via hard-to-reverse rails (bank transfer, crypto ATM, gift cards, cash/gold courier, OTP read-back). Pushback is met with escalation (threat of arrest, "sheriff to your doorstep"), a supervisor, or a quick callback.

### Cited Findings

**Cross-type structure (academic corpora)**
- HMM analysis of 341 scambaiter transcripts (~90 hours) found a common high-level structure: greeting, explanation of the problem, instructions, then a financial exploitation attempt; SSA scams add a "redirection to supervisor" step; casual chit-chat often precedes payment requests. Average transcript ~90.7 utterances, median 82, roughly 15-16 minutes per call — [Eren et al., arXiv 2307.01965](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01965v1)
- 10,211 inbound honeypot calls (913 hours): thirty opening clusters account for traffic, top five carry about half; callers request identity anchors (home address, DOB) far more often than payment credentials; they rely on "persistence and manufactured authority rather than overt threats"; escalation predictable at 0.72 ROC-AUC from the first line and 0.87 by the eighth; 6.6x more calls per weekday than weekend day; scammers spent ~15% more turns per decade of apparent target age — [Traister et al., arXiv 2608.24127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24127)
- FTC: scammers "tag team" victims, e.g., a fake Microsoft pop-up transfers to someone posing as an FTC employee; they impersonate the FTC (sometimes using real staff names), banks, Microsoft and SSA; they keep victims on the phone to build fear and prevent consulting family; transfers are justified as keeping money safe, securing identity, "clear your name," or helping catch criminals — [FTC Data Spotlight, Aug 2025](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings)

**A. Government impersonation: SSA / "your SSN is linked to a crime"**
- Opening greeting is cheerful ("Hello. And thank you for calling…"); scammers claim "officer" roles at SSA; they use anger to stop interruptions: "Do not interrupt me in between..." and "...suspend your social for interrupting the officer…"; they ask whether the victim's identity was stolen (driver's license, SS card); describe crime scenes with stolen cars, drugs, blood and lines like "...these are now used to launder approximately $240,000..."; verify identity ("...Can you verify me the last four digits of social security number?..."); provide a case ID ("...This is the case identification number…"); redirect to a "senior agent"; threaten "...I will send a local sheriff on your doorstep…" and "...I'm sending the cops to your house…" — [arXiv 2307.01965](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01965v1)
- FTC description of the lie: a government officer warns that your Social Security number is linked to drug smuggling, money laundering, or child pornography — [FTC Data Spotlight, Aug 2025](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings)

**B. Government impersonation: jury duty / warrant (AARP case, Florida 2025)**
- Caller claimed to be an officer from the county courthouse, already knew the victim's name, address, DOB, SSN, driver's license number and car; emails/texts with court logos and case numbers arrived during the call; crisis: missed jury duty yesterday, warrant for failure to appear and contempt; demanded a $9,000 bond, then said $1,000 was not enough after a "red flag"; victim was directed to withdraw cash and deposit at crypto ATMs (QR code displayed "Pasco Sheriff's Office"); told she could not hang up or talk to anyone, caller knew when she texted her son; a "sergeant" was said to be monitoring her drive; when she questioned not entering her number on the receipt the caller got angry, louder, and threatened to dispatch an officer; a second person was brought in for "approval"; when her husband hung up the scammers called right back and warned her not to do that again; the ordeal lasted about three hours; closing line: "You can go home now," warrant "frozen" — [AARP The Perfect Scam, 2025](https://www.aarp.org/podcasts/the-perfect-scam/info-2025/jury-duty-crypto-atm-scam.html)
- Quoted pressure line: "If you don't do this right now, you're going to get arrested." — [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/podcasts/the-perfect-scam/info-2025/jury-duty-crypto-atm-scam.html)

**C. Government/bank impersonation: gold bar and cash courier variant**
- Victims are persuaded to put funds in a "government-owned" bank account or hand cash or gold to a courier sent to their address; takes the form of grandparent, government impersonation, or tech support schemes; nationwide 2023-2025: at least 1,737 instances using couriers, approximately $186.2M+ in losses; FBI Boston Division: 103 instances, $26M+; 98% of losses reported by people over 60; FBI: "the government will never ask people to purchase gold or precious metals" — [Agency Checklists summary of FBI Boston alert, Sept 2025](https://agencychecklists.com/2025/09/25/fbi-alert-sounds-alarm-on-costly-elder-fraud-scams-77179/)
- FTC: gold was mentioned in about 5% of older adults' $10K+ loss reports and about 21% of $100K+ reports in 2024 — [FTC Imposter Data Spotlight PDF, Aug 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Imposter-Data-Spotlight-August-2025.pdf)

**D. Bank "fraud department" impersonation / OTP and Zelle "pay yourself"**
- Caller ID shows the bank's name; caller introduces themselves as the fraud department, says suspicious activity was detected, already knows name, bank and some account details; asks "Did you authorize a $1,200 charge?"; after "no," says the account must be secured right away; "We're going to send you a security code. Please read it back to me so we can verify your identity." The code is actually an account-access/password-reset code — [Reliabank, "When the Fraud Department Is the Fraud"](https://www.reliabank.com/when-the-fraud-department-is-the-fraud/)
- Zelle "pay yourself" variant: starts with a text that looks like a bank fraud alert; replying triggers a call from a spoofed bank number; caller offers to stop the fraud and tells you to send money "to yourself"; claims they need the one-time enrollment code to authorize it, then enrolls their own account under your phone/email; variant: send to an email address containing your name plus "Zelle Dispute," the bank name, "Fraud," or "Claim" — [Zelle](https://www.zelle.com/how-to-spot-a-pay-yourself-scam)

**E. Tech support and the FBI "Phantom Hacker" three-phase script**
- Phase 1 (tech support imposter): contact via pop-up, call, text or email directing victim to call a number for "assistance"; victim downloads remote-access software; scammer runs a fake virus scan, claims the computer was or is at risk of being hacked; asks victim to open financial accounts to check for unauthorized charges (used to pick the most lucrative account); tells victim the institution's "fraud department" will call. Phase 2 (financial institution imposter): claims a foreign hacker accessed the accounts; instructs moving money to a "safe" third-party account, e.g., at the Federal Reserve or another US government agency; may require multiple transfers over days or months; victim told not to tell anyone why they are moving money. Phase 3 (US government imposter): Federal Reserve/government employee reinforces the claim; if victim gets suspicious, sends email/letter on what looks like official letterhead; keeps pressing to move funds to an "alias" account. Payment: wire, cash, or crypto, often overseas. Jan-June 2023: 19,000 tech support complaints, $542M+ losses, ~50% of victims over 60 accounting for 66% of losses — [FBI IC3 PSA I-091223 (Sept 2023)](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2023/PSA230929)
- FBI (Aug 2025): the scam "has cost Americans over $1 billion since at least 2024"; a Check Point executive estimated same-day reporting gives a roughly 10-15% recovery chance — [FOX 5 DC, Aug 2025](https://www.fox5dc.com/news/fbi-warns-seniors-about-devastating-cyber-scam-wipes-out-life-savings)
- FTC: older adults were five times more likely than younger people to report losing money to tech support scams; $159M in 2024 losses for 60+ — [FTC Older Adults Report Dec 2025 (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P144400-OlderAdultsReportDec2025.pdf)

**F. Amazon / package impersonation (robocall-to-agent refund script)**
- Robocall opening: "Thank you for shopping with Amazon." / "Your iPhone purchase has been confirmed." / "A total amount of $1,299 will be charged to your account." / "If you did not authorize it, press 1 to speak with customer support."; live agent poses as Amazon billing/fraud prevention with a case number; harvests name, address, email, DOB, bank/card digits: "Before I can cancel the order, I need to verify the account holder."; OTP request: "Read it to me so I can cancel the iPhone order."; pivot to browser/"cancellation form" then remote access; fake refund processed while victim is logged into online banking; overpayment ploy: "We refunded $2,999 instead of $1,299."; payment via gift cards, wire, money transfer, crypto or P2P; isolation: "Do not call your bank yet."; follow-on calls from fake supervisors, bank fraud teams or "recovery services" — [MalwareTips investigation](https://malwaretips.com/blogs/amazon-iphone-purchase-phone-call-scam-exposed-full-investigation/)
- Academic corpus refund-scam lines: "This is suspicious activity on the Amazon account…" and "So let me help you to get connected with our Amazon…" followed by device info and remote-desktop install instructions; payment via Amazon gift cards — [arXiv 2307.01965](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01965v1)

**G. Family emergency / grandparent**
- Opening: a distressed voice says "Grandma, it's me," often with an excuse for why the voice sounds different; caller may ask the grandparent to guess who is calling so the grandparent supplies the name; crises: arrest, car crash, hospital; a second person posing as a lawyer, police officer or doctor takes over and quotes a bail, fee or fine; asks the grandparent not to tell parents or relatives; demands wire transfer, prepaid gift cards, or a courier/"bond agent" collecting cash at the door; invented deadline — [AOL/police warning](https://www.aol.com/articles/police-warn-family-emergency-scam-174518309.html)
- AI variant (Poway, CA, June 2026): first caller claimed to be law enforcement with an arrest warrant, then a woman who "sounded exactly like his daughter" came on; couple withdrew $18,000 and handed it to a courier in a CVS parking lot — [Hoodline](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/poway-dad-says-ai-cloned-daughter-s-voice-wiped-out-life-savings/)
- IC3 2025: distress ("grandparent") scams using voice cloning had claimed losses over $5M in 2025 — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)

**H. Virtual kidnapping (AI voice)**
- Callers claim a loved one has been kidnapped, play what sounds like that person begging, then demand fast payment; one Houston victim wired $5,000; "proof of life" media often edited and set to disappear; red flags per FBI: odd pauses, strange pitch changes, pressure not to verify — [Hoodline, June 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/ai-fake-kidnap-calls-rattle-houston-families-fbi-warns/)

**I. Pig butchering (investment) lead-in**
- First contact looks accidental or social: wrong-number text, social media message, dating app match, or networking request; rapport built "over days or weeks," chat moved to a private messaging app; scammer mentions crypto trading success and offers to teach; fake exchange shows large gains; a small withdrawal may be allowed; larger withdrawals blocked by demands for "taxes, fees, or penalties" — [Scam Atlas (secondary)](https://guptadeepak.com/scam-atlas/scam/pig-butchering/)
- Messages are sent via WhatsApp, text or dating apps "as if it was intended for someone else," often with an attractive profile photo; emotional hooks such as "enough money for your kids"; withdrawal fee at exit — [NBC New York](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/business/money-report/got-a-text-from-a-wrong-number-it-could-be-an-attempt-at-pig-butchering-a-crypto-scam-costing-investors-millions/3839072/)

**J. Medicare / health**
- Pretexts and quoted phrasings: new Medicare card being issued (false; cards reissued in 2018); coverage about to be cancelled, must verify identity; "You're preapproved for a better, cheaper plan."; "eligible for a refund for overpayment of benefits." (to get banking info); "You qualify for free medical supplies." (back/knee braces, genetic testing); "Our plan is preferred by Medicare."; asks for Medicare number, DOB, SSN, banking info; "Medicare will not call you to verify or update your Medicare number or to enroll you in a plan." — [Washington OIC Medicare fraud fact sheet, Oct 2025 (PDF)](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/medicare-fraud-oep-fact-sheet.pdf)
- Hiya: Q4 2024 Medicare scams sought personal details to enroll recipients in hospice and bill Medicare for home health services — [Hiya Q4 2024](https://www.hiya.com/newsroom/press-releases/ai-deepfake-fraud-calls-dominate-q4-scams-costing-consumers-millions)

**K. Phantom debt collection**
- Callers pose as a law firm (sometimes under several names), claim a payday loan or other debt, cite a lawsuit, summons or court case with a case number and callback number, threaten arrest or jail; consumer-quoted lines: "if I didn't validate my full social and pay the fee, I would have an arrest warrant out"; "I have four hours to settle up and pay the account in full"; "I will see you in court"; "I'm going to serve you documents on November 19 from 3-5pm if you do not sign"; payment demanded by card over the phone, wire, prepaid cards, or "delivery" fees for court documents; FTC: "You can't be arrested for not paying your bills," and real collectors must send a validation notice within five days — [FTC consumer alert and comments](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2017/08/phantom-debt-collectors-impersonate-law-firms?page=5)

**L. Prize / sweepstakes / lottery**
- Ohio case (2025): caller posed as a sweepstakes employee invoking Publishers Clearing House, claimed a $3 million prize; "All she needed to do, he said, was pay taxes on her winnings"; victim mailed checks labeled "tax" to an Arkansas address, lost more than $89,000 — [Yahoo/Moneywise](https://www.yahoo.com/news/ohio-woman-76-swindled-89k-110900414.html)
- IC3 2025 lottery/sweepstakes/inheritance: 5,623 complaints, $194.1M (up from $102.2M in 2024) — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)
- For phone-initiated fraud among older adults, prize/sweepstakes accounted for 15% of dollar losses in 2024 (business impersonation 40%, government impersonation 23%) — [FTC Older Adults Report Dec 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P144400-OlderAdultsReportDec2025.pdf)

**M. Utility shutoff**
- Caller claims to represent the utility, may quote service address and partial account detail; crisis: past-due balance, failed payment, meter replacement fee, or final disconnection order "within minutes or hours," a truck already nearby, fake work-order number, deadline after which a technician "cannot be recalled"; payment via gift cards, wire, crypto kiosks, QR codes/barcodes paid at a retailer, or payment apps, amount "chosen to feel serious but still payable under pressure"; a "supervisor or disconnection department" continues pressure; if victim says they will call the utility directly the caller may become hostile; after a first payment the caller may claim it failed or a second fee is due — [MalwareTips utility scam writeup](https://malwaretips.com/blogs/?p=402713)

### Inferences
- Detector-relevant lexical/semantic signals recur across archetypes: "case number / case ID," "verify the last four of your Social," "do not hang up / stay on the line," "do not tell anyone / do not call your bank yet," "read me the code," "safe account / protect your money," "suspended," "warrant / failure to appear," "sheriff / officer dispatched," "press 1 to dispute," "refund / overpayment," "gift card / Bitcoin ATM / courier," and transfers to a "supervisor" or second "department."
- Call duration spans a wide range: robocall-to-agent refund and Medicare calls are minutes; scambaiter-recorded SSA/refund calls average ~15 minutes; high-loss government/jury-duty and Phantom Hacker scripts run hours to months with repeated callbacks. A detector should score escalation early (honeypot study shows first-line predictiveness) rather than wait for payment language.
- Pushback handling is itself a signal: legitimate agencies allow you to hang up and call back; scam scripts respond to verification attempts with anger, threats, a supervisor, or an immediate callback.

### Gaps
- No primary-source (FTC/FBI) verbatim transcripts exist for most types; verbatim lines above come from an academic scambaiter corpus, a consumer-education site (MalwareTips), bank education pages, and AARP victim narratives. The FTC testimony and spotlights describe patterns but include no scripts.
- Typical call length by type is not published by FTC/FBI; only the academic averages (~15 min scambaiter calls, ~5 min honeypot calls by calculation) and single-case anecdotes (3 hours for jury duty) are available.
- No current authoritative script surfaced for toll/DMV smishing-to-call or for "IRS" specifically (SSA has largely displaced IRS in recent corpora, but this was not quantified).

## Key Question 3: AI voice cloning and deepfake audio in scam calls (2025-2026) and detection implications

### Takeaway
Voice cloning is confirmed in the wild by the FBI (official-impersonation vishing since April 2025; grandparent and kidnapping variants) but remains a small share of reported dollar losses (IC3: ~$5M grandparent/voice-clone, $19M romance-with-AI, $893M across all 22,000+ AI-referenced complaints). Vendor data (Pindrop) show steep growth in synthetic-voice attacks on contact centers. The FBI's own detection guidance is behavioral (call lag, voice mismatch, odd tone or word choice) plus a family "secret word," which implies a call detector should weight script and pressure signals over pure acoustic authenticity.

### Cited Findings
- FBI PSA (May 15, 2025): since April 2025 malicious actors have impersonated senior US officials via text and AI-generated voice messages targeting current/former senior federal and state officials and their contacts; goal is rapport, then access to personal accounts, then impersonating those contacts to request information or money; technique includes moving to a secondary messaging app to deliver a malicious link; FBI detection tips for voice: "call lag, voice mismatches, and unusual tone or word choice," noting AI clones "can sound nearly identical to real voices"; recommends verifying via an independently found number, never sharing 2FA codes, and agreeing on a secret word or phrase with family — [FBI IC3 PSA 250515](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250515)
- IC3 2025: more than 22,000 AI-referenced complaints with adjusted losses above $893M; "Voice cloning can also be used to request wire payment" in BEC; confidence/romance scams with a likely AI nexus caused over $19M; distress ("grandparent") scams using voice cloning claimed over $5M; employment scams with voice spoofing in interviews almost $13M — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf); 22,364 AI complaints / ~$893.3M figure also cited by [Hoodline, June 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/ai-fake-kidnap-calls-rattle-houston-families-fbi-warns/)
- Pindrop 2025 Voice Intelligence & Security Report (vendor data, 2024 observations): deepfake fraud attempts rose more than 1,300% (from about one per month to about seven per day); synthetic voice calls rose 173% Q1 to Q4; synthetic voice attacks up 475% in insurance and 149% in banking; retail fraud rate 1 attempt per 127 calls; 2025 projections: deepfaked calls +155%, deepfake-related fraud +162% — [CIO Influence summary of Pindrop report](https://cioinfluence.com/security/pindrops-2025-voice-intelligence-security-report-reveals-1300-surge-in-deepfake-fraud/)
- Hiya consumer survey (Jan 2025, 12,000 respondents in six countries): 31% of US consumers reported encountering deepfake fraud calls; more victims reported losses above $6,000 from deepfake fraud calls than from traditional phone scams; across six countries over one-third encountered a deepfake fraud call and over 30% of those targeted became victims — [Hiya Q4 2024 press release](https://www.hiya.com/newsroom/press-releases/ai-deepfake-fraud-calls-dominate-q4-scams-costing-consumers-millions)
- A believable clone can be built from a few seconds of audio taken from social media or voicemail; FBI red flags: odd pauses, strange pitch changes, pressure not to verify; edited "proof of life" media often set to disappear — [Hoodline, June 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/ai-fake-kidnap-calls-rattle-houston-families-fbi-warns/)
- Case examples: Poway, CA father lost $18,000 cash to a courier after a cloned "daughter" voice followed a fake law-enforcement caller — [Hoodline, June 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/poway-dad-says-ai-cloned-daughter-s-voice-wiped-out-life-savings/); Houston mother wired $5,000 after hearing what sounded like her daughter's voice in a fake kidnapping — [Hoodline, June 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/06/ai-fake-kidnap-calls-rattle-houston-families-fbi-warns/)
- The FTC ran a Voice Cloning Challenge (2023-2024) aimed at AI voice-cloning harms — [FTC JEC testimony](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-testimony-jec-hearing-on-the-rising-scam-economy.pdf)

### Inferences
- The script around a cloned voice is unchanged (crisis + authority handoff + secrecy + untraceable payment); the clone only removes the "voice sounds different" tell. A detector trained on conversational structure should still fire on cloned-voice family emergency calls.
- Reported dollar losses attributable to voice cloning are small relative to total impersonation losses, but the trajectory data (Pindrop, Hiya) and FBI warnings suggest this is a growing, high-emotional-salience vector that is useful for product narrative.
- Acoustic deepfake detection is possible but not required for a first-pass consumer product; FBI's recommended defenses (hang up, call back, secret word) are behavioral, which a product can prompt in real time.

### Gaps
- No independent (non-vendor) measurement of how many inbound US consumer scam calls use synthetic voice; Pindrop and Hiya are vendors with products to sell and their methodology is not detailed in the retrieved summaries.
- The FTC has not published a voice-cloning-specific loss figure.

## Key Question 4: Demographics of who loses the most (older vs younger, by scam type)

### Takeaway
Younger adults report losing money more often, but older adults lose far more per incident and dominate the catastrophic-loss tail: 60+ filed $7.7B of IC3's $20.9B in 2025 losses (average $38,500 per complaint), and FTC 80+ median loss was $1,650 versus $189 for teens. Phone is the top channel for 80+, with the highest median loss of any channel for older adults ($2,210). Older adults are disproportionately hit by tech support (5x), government impersonation (+36%), romance (+39%), prize/sweepstakes, and the gold/cash-courier variant (98% of losses from 60+).

### Cited Findings
- FBI IC3 2025 losses by age: under 20: 31,254 complaints / $67.1M; 20-29: 112,069 / $563.1M; 30-39: 153,293 / $1.7B; 40-49: 167,066 / $2.957B; 50-59: 124,820 / $3.7B; 60+: 201,266 / $7.7B — [FBI IC3 2025 Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)
- 60+ in IC3 2025: complaints up 37% YoY, losses $7.748B up 59% YoY, average loss $38,500, 12,444 victims losing over $100,000; top losses for 60+: investment $3.5B, tech support $1.04B, confidence/romance $584M, government impersonation $413M; crypto-related losses $4.43B — [SOCRadar summary of IC3 2025](https://socradar.io/blog/fbi-ic3-2025-internet-crime-report-10-takeaways/)
- FTC 2024 (60+): reported losses nearly $2.4B, up from ~$1.9B in 2023 and ~$600M in 2020; estimated true cost $10.1B to $81.5B; 74% of older adults' fraud reports indicated no monetary loss; older adults lost money at a lower rate than younger adults; median individual loss $900 for 60+ (up from $650) and $1,650 for 80+; reports of $100,000+ losses: 5,125 in 2024 vs 1,136 in 2020, representing 5% of loss reports but 68% of aggregate losses; phone-initiated median loss $2,210 vs $650 for social media; older adults 5x more likely to report losing money to tech support ($159M), 39% more likely for romance, 36% more likely for government impersonation; for phone-initiated fraud, business impersonation = 40% of losses, government impersonation 23%, prize/sweepstakes 15% — [FTC Protecting Older Consumers 2024-2025 (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P144400-OlderAdultsReportDec2025.pdf)
- FTC: 51% of complaints from consumers 19 and younger reported losing money versus 21% from those 80+; median loss $189 (19 and under) vs $1,650 (80+) — [Fraud.org citing FTC](https://fraud.org/?p=4114)
- FTC 2024, older adults reporting $10K+ losses to business/government impersonation: first contact by phone call 41%, online ad/pop-up 15%, email 13%; payment: crypto 33% (mostly Bitcoin ATMs), bank transfer 20%, cash 16%; for $100K+ losses bank transfer was most common at 32%; older adults were more than twice as likely to report $10K+ losses to impersonators and three times as likely to report $100K+ losses (population-normalized); counts of $10K+ older-adult impersonation reports: 1,790 (2020), 3,516 (2021), 5,559 (2022), 7,091 (2023), 8,269 (2024); $100K+ combined losses rose from $55M (2020) to $445M (2024) — [FTC Data Spotlight, Aug 2025](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings); [FTC press release Aug 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-data-show-more-four-fold-increase-reports-impersonation-scammers-stealing-tens-even-hundreds)
- FTC 2025 contact-method by age: phone call share of first contact rises steadily from about age 60 while social media's share falls; adults 80+ most often report a phone call as first contact; social media's share of loss reports: 40% (18-29) down to 14% (80+); for 80+, phone call was the most costly contact method in aggregate — [FTC Data Spotlight April 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2026/04/reported-losses-scams-social-media-eight-times-higher-2020); [Stacker summary of FTC data](https://stacker.com/stories/financial-protection/scams-targeting-you-most-age-group)
- FTC 2024 losses by age group: 60-69 ~$1.18B, 50-59 ~$1.006B, 40-49 ~$971M, 70-79 ~$887M, 30-39 ~$810M, 20-29 ~$430M, 80+ ~$319M, 19 and under ~$55M — [ABA Business Law Today (secondary)](https://businesslawtoday.org/2026/08/consumer-sentinel-data-business-law-tool-measuring-consumer-financial-harm/)
- Gold/cash courier scams: 98% of losses reported by people over 60 — [Agency Checklists / FBI Boston](https://agencychecklists.com/2025/09/25/fbi-alert-sounds-alarm-on-costly-elder-fraud-scams-77179/)
- Honeypot experiment: scammers spent ~15% more conversational turns per decade of the target's apparent age, but requested sensitive info at the same rate (26.3% of calls) regardless of age — [arXiv 2608.24127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24127)
- Truecaller (2021): younger people more susceptible than older people; men fall for phone scams more often than women across all age groups — [TechSpot summary](https://www.techspot.com/news/90702-phone-scammers-have-swindled-americans-out-nearly-30.html)
- AARP survey: 41% of adults had money or sensitive information stolen and used fraudulently; only 21% of victims reported to local law enforcement — [Fraud.org citing AARP](https://fraud.org/?p=4114)

### Inferences
- The protective-product pitch has two segments: (1) adults 60+ (especially 80+) where phone is the primary channel and losses are catastrophic (median $2,210 per phone-initiated incident, 68% of older-adult losses from $100K+ events); (2) adult children who want to protect parents, since older victims under-report and are isolated by the scammer during the call.
- The "younger people get scammed more often" finding is real but concerns low-dollar online shopping and job scams, not phone impersonation; it is a weaker argument for a call detector.

### Gaps
- FTC 2024 median losses for 60-69 and 70-79 brackets, and dollar figures by fraud type for older adults, are in report charts not retrieved as text.
- No source provided gender breakdowns for 2024-2026 phone scam losses.

## Key Question 5: Published collections of real or realistic scam call transcripts or audio

### Takeaway
Two academic corpora are directly relevant: a 341-transcript (~90 hour) scambaiter YouTube corpus with HMM-derived script stages (2023) and a 10,211-call (913 hour) AI-honeypot corpus with 330,956 transcribed turns (Aug 2026). Public availability of the raw data is not confirmed for either. FTC and FBI publish narrative descriptions and consumer-comment quotes but no transcript datasets; AARP's Perfect Scam podcast provides detailed victim narratives with quoted lines.

### Cited Findings
- Eren et al. (2023), "An analysis of scam baiting calls: Identifying and extracting scam stages and scripts": 341 transcripts, ~90 hours, from ~825 candidate scambaiter YouTube videos (about 60% excluded); transcribed with rev.ai and manually speaker-labeled (Scammer/Victim); types: SSN (~138-140), refund (110), support (63), reward/gift card (~13-25), with 1-2 each of family member, tax, charity; dataset URL was a placeholder "to be released on acceptance," so public access is unconfirmed — [arXiv 2307.01965](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01965v1)
- Traister et al. (Aug 2026), "Anatomy of a Scam Call": 10,211 inbound scam and spam calls, 913 hours, 330,956 transcribed turns, 5,780 distinct numbers, 54 days, AI voice-agent honeypot; mentions a companion data descriptor but the abstract page does not state public release — [arXiv 2608.24127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24127)
- A privacy-preserving real-time scam detection and conversational scambaiting paper using LLMs and federated learning exists (Sept 2025) — [arXiv 2509.05362](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.05362) (not fetched; title from search result only)
- A scam-call honeypot age experiment writeup exists from Pebblous — [Pebblous blog](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/scam-call-honeypot-age-experiment/en/) (not fetched; from search result only)
- AARP "The Perfect Scam" podcast publishes long-form victim narratives with quoted scammer lines (e.g., jury duty / crypto ATM episode, 2025) — [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/podcasts/the-perfect-scam/info-2025/jury-duty-crypto-atm-scam.html)
- FTC consumer alert comment threads contain first-person quotes of caller wording (debt collection) — [consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2017/08/phantom-debt-collectors-impersonate-law-firms?page=5)
- FTC Data Spotlights describe script patterns ("Someone is using your accounts," "clear your name," "protect it") but contain no verbatim consumer narratives — [FTC Data Spotlight Aug 2025](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings)
- FBI IC3 PSAs give phase-by-phase script structure (Phantom Hacker; official impersonation) but no transcripts — [IC3 PSA 2023](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2023/PSA230929); [IC3 PSA 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250515)

### Inferences
- For synthetic transcript generation, the most defensible references are the HMM stage structure and quoted utterances from arXiv 2307.01965 (SSA and refund), the FBI Phantom Hacker phases, the MalwareTips Amazon sequence, and AARP's jury duty narrative. Scambaiter YouTube channels (the source of the 2023 corpus) remain the largest open pool of real scammer audio, with the caveat that the "victim" side is a baiter, not a real target.
- Any claim that a public labeled scam-call audio dataset exists should be checked against the arXiv papers' data statements before citing.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm a downloadable public release for either academic corpus; the 2023 paper's data link was a placeholder and the 2026 abstract page does not state availability.
- No FTC/FBI-published transcript or audio corpus was found.
- Did not retrieve scambaiter channel names or specific transcribed episodes (e.g., Kitboga, Jim Browning) from a citable source within this session; these channels are the raw material behind arXiv 2307.01965 but were not independently sourced here.
